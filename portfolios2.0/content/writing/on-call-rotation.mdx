Most on-call horror stories aren't about too many incidents. They're about too
many *pages* — the pager crying wolf until people mute it, and then a real
incident slips through the muted noise.

## The starting point

We had no rotation. Incidents landed on whoever happened to be looking, which in
practice meant the same two people every time. That's not a rotation, it's a slow
way to lose your best engineers.

## The three changes that mattered

1. **Alert on symptoms, not causes.** We stopped paging on CPU and memory and
   started paging on error-budget burn. A blip that self-resolves is a dashboard
   line, not a 3am phone call.
2. **Every alert links a runbook.** If a page doesn't come with "here's what this
   means and the first thing to check," it's not ready to page anyone.
3. **Blameless postmortems, always.** The output of an incident is a fix and a
   changed system — never a name.

## What changed

Page volume dropped by about two-thirds, and the pages that survived were real.
The rotation became something people trusted instead of dreaded — which, it turns
out, is the whole point.

> A pager you can trust is worth more than a dashboard you have to babysit.
